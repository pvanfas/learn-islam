# Sufis and Scholars as Carriers of Islam

Difficulty: Intermediate | Part: Part 12 — How Islam Spread

---

If merchants were Islam's first missionaries, Sufi shaykhs and traveling scholars were its most effective ones. They reached populations that commerce alone could not touch — the rural poor, the spiritual seekers, the communities far from trade routes.

The Sufi approach to da'wah was fundamentally different from political conquest. The shaykh arrived not as a conqueror but as a healer — treating the sick, mediating disputes, providing education, and demonstrating a spiritual depth that commanded respect. Where rulers converted and expected their subjects to follow, the Sufi shaykh worked person by person, community by community, across generations.

The Qadiri order, founded by Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (d. 1166 CE) in Baghdad, became perhaps the most geographically widespread Islamic institution in history — with lodges from Morocco to Indonesia, from West Africa to Central Asia. Each lodge was a center of education, spiritual guidance, and social service.

The traveling scholar (alim) was equally significant. Ibn Battuta's extraordinary 14th-century journey — 75,000 miles across 44 modern countries over 29 years — was not exceptional in its purpose, only in its scale. Muslim scholars routinely traveled from Andalusia to Central Asia, from West Africa to China, carrying books, issuing legal opinions, and strengthening the connections that made the Muslim world a genuine civilization rather than a collection of disconnected political entities.

The result of this patient, personal, spiritual da'wah: Islam is today the majority religion in more countries than any other faith — and the dominant religion of populations that no Muslim army ever conquered.
