## Why Sects Emerged

The Prophet said: "My Ummah will split into 73 sects, all in the Fire except one." He was asked which is the saved one and replied: "The one that follows what I and my Companions are upon." (Tirmidhi Hasan)

Sectarian divisions emerged from three main sources: political disputes over succession, theological disagreements about the nature of God and free will, and legal differences in deriving rulings.

---

## The Main Divisions

### Sunni (Ahlus Sunnah Wal Jama'ah)

The majority approximately 85-90% of Muslims globally. Defined by adherence to the Sunnah of the Prophet and the way of the Companions, following one of the four recognized madhabs in law, and one of the three recognized schools in theology (Ash'ari, Maturidi, Athari).

Sunnis accept all four of the first caliphs Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali as legitimate, and regard the Companions collectively as trustworthy transmitters of the religion.

### Shi'a

Approximately 10-15% of Muslims globally. The word Shi'a comes from "Shi'at Ali" the party of Ali. The core Shi'a position: Ali ibn Abi Talib was the rightful successor to the Prophet immediately after his death, and his descendants (the Imams) have special spiritual authority over the Muslim community.

The largest Shi'a group is the Ithna Ashariyyah (Twelvers), who believe in twelve Imams, the last of whom (Muhammad al-Mahdi) went into occultation in 874 CE and will return. This is the dominant religion in Iran, Iraq, and Bahrain.

Major differences from Sunni Islam include: the role and authority of the Imams, the status of the Companions (some of whom Shi'a regard as having betrayed Ali), and certain legal differences.

### Ibadi

A small, distinct group neither Sunni nor Shi'a primarily in Oman, Zanzibar, and parts of North Africa. They trace their origins to the early Kharijite movement but distinguish themselves from the Kharijites' violent extremism. They have their own legal tradition and theological positions.

### Kharijites (Historical)

The first schismatic movement in Islamic history they broke from Ali (RA) after the Battle of Siffin and declared all who committed major sins to be apostates deserving death. They were defeated militarily, but their ideology of declaring Muslims as disbelievers (takfir) for political or religious deviation has recurred throughout history.

Contemporary jihadist movements that practice takfir against Muslim governments and civilians trace intellectual lineage to Kharijite methodology, even if they do not use the name.

---

## The Sunni Position on Other Groups

Ahlus Sunnah does not declare Shi'a or Ibadi Muslims as non-Muslims (kafir) these are Muslim communities with whom Sunnis have serious theological disagreements. Productive dialogue, clear scholarly engagement with differences, and avoidance of violence are the Sunni approach.

What Ahlus Sunnah does reject: the theological positions of the Mu'tazilah (who denied certain divine attributes), the extreme Shi'a positions that amount to veneration of the Imams beyond Islamic bounds, and Kharijite-style takfir.
