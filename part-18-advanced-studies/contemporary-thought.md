# Contemporary Islamic Thought — Renewal, Reform, and Modernity

## 📌 In This Chapter

> The 18th–21st centuries saw Islam face unprecedented challenges — colonialism, Western modernity, secularism, nationalism, and the crisis of Muslim political fragmentation. This chapter surveys the major thinkers and movements that have shaped modern Islamic thought — from revivalist reformers to modernist scholars — presenting their ideas and the ongoing debates they generated.

---

## 🧭 The Context — Why Modern Islamic Thought Emerged

By the 18th century, the Muslim world faced a crisis. The great empires (Ottoman, Mughal, Safavid) were in decline. European colonial powers were systematically dominating Muslim territories. The question confronting Muslim thinkers was urgent: *Why has the Muslim world declined, and what is the path to renewal?*

Three broad answers emerged:

1. **Revivalist:** Return to the pure sources (Quran and Sunnah) — the decline is caused by deviation from authentic Islam
2. **Reformist:** Reform Islamic thought to engage productively with modernity — without abandoning Islamic principles
3. **Modernist:** Reinterpret Islam substantially to accommodate modern values

## 🌟 Major Thinkers and Movements

---

### Shah Waliullah al-Dehlawi (1703–1762 CE) — India

**The great synthesizer.** Born in Delhi during the Mughal Empire's decline, Shah Waliullah attempted to reconcile the four madhabs, bridge the gap between Sufism and hadith-based scholarship, and produce an integrated Islamic intellectual framework for a Muslim minority navigating the loss of political power.

**Key contributions:**
- Translated the Quran into Persian — making it accessible to the educated non-Arabic speakers of South Asia
- Argued for *Ijtihad* (independent scholarly reasoning) after centuries of what he saw as excessive *Taqlid* (blind following)
- Reconciled the Ash'ari and Maturidi theological schools
- His students would go on to found the Deobandi movement, one of the most influential Islamic movements in South Asia

**His legacy:** The entire intellectual tradition of South Asian Islamic scholarship traces itself to Shah Waliullah.

---

### Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (1703–1792 CE) — Arabia

**The reformer of the Arabian Peninsula.** A Hanbali scholar who saw the Muslim world of his time as filled with bid'ah, grave worship, and practices he considered Shirk. He allied with Muhammad ibn Saud — the political ruler of Najd — in an effort to purify Arabian Islam and return it to what he understood as the practice of the Salaf.

**Key contributions:**
- Focused on purifying Tawhid from what he saw as innovations and Shirk
- *Kitab al-Tawhid* — his primary text on monotheism
- His alliance with the House of Saud established what eventually became the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia

**The controversy:** His movement destroyed historical sites associated with the Prophet ﷺ and the Companions, viewing them as potential focal points for veneration. This is considered by many Muslims — including Ahlus Sunnah scholars — as excessive. His critics argue he misclassified legitimate scholarly practices as Shirk, while his supporters argue he was correct to take a strict position on Tawhid.

**His legacy:** The *Wahhabi* or *Salafi* movement — with Saudi funding — became globally influential in the 20th century.

---

### Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838–1897 CE) — Pan-Islamism

**The political activist.** Born in Afghanistan, al-Afghani traveled across the Muslim world arguing for Pan-Islamic unity as a response to European colonialism. He believed Muslims needed to modernize intellectually while resisting Western political domination.

**Key positions:**
- Islamic rationalism — arguing that Islam is fully compatible with reason and science
- Pan-Islamic political solidarity across national borders
- Anti-colonialism — resistance to European imperialism

**His influence:** He inspired generations of Islamic modernist thinkers, including Muhammad Abduh.

---

### Muhammad Abduh (1849–1905 CE) — Egypt

**The rationalist reformer.** Grand Mufti of Egypt and one of the most consequential Islamic modernist thinkers, Abduh argued for a rational reinterpretation of Islam that could engage productively with Western science and political philosophy.

**Key positions:**
- Reason and revelation are compatible — Islamic theology should not fear rational inquiry
- Many traditional rulings need reinterpretation (*Tajdid*) for modern contexts
- Islamic education needed radical reform
- Taqlid (blind following of madhabs) needed to be reduced in favor of direct engagement with sources

**The criticism:** Some of his students and followers went further than Abduh intended — toward positions that many scholars consider reductive of Islamic tradition.

---

### Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938 CE) — South Asia

**The poet-philosopher.** Perhaps the greatest Islamic thinker in the Urdu and Persian languages. Iqbal combined deep engagement with Western philosophy (he studied in Germany and England) with a profound Islamic worldview to produce a vision of Islamic renewal.

**Key contributions:**
- *Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* (1930) — his major philosophical work arguing for the renewal of Islamic theology through engagement with modern philosophy
- Poetry (*Bang-i-Dara, Bal-i-Jibril, Javid-nama*) — among the most widely read in South Asian history; his poetry revived Islamic identity among Muslims across the subcontinent
- Political vision — he is considered the intellectual father of Pakistan

**His central idea:** *Khudi* (self) — the Islamic individual must develop their inner spiritual strength (*selfhood*) rooted in divine relationship, rather than being swallowed by either Western materialism or an inert traditionalism.

---

### Hassan al-Banna (1906–1949 CE) — Egypt

**The organizer.** Founder of the *Muslim Brotherhood* (Al-Ikhwan al-Muslimun) — the first and most influential modern Islamic political organization, founded in Egypt in 1928.

**Key positions:**
- Islam is a comprehensive system — *al-Islam din wa dawlah* (Islam is religion and state)
- Gradual, educational, and political approach to Islamic renewal — not violent revolution
- Da'wah through community organizations — schools, clinics, and social services
- Opposition to colonialism and Western cultural domination

**His influence:** The Brotherhood became the template for virtually all subsequent Sunni Islamic political movements globally — from Hamas to Ennahda to the AKP.

**His end:** He was assassinated by Egyptian state agents in 1949.

---

### Sayyid Abul Ala Maududi (1903–1979 CE) — Pakistan

**The systematizer.** Founder of *Jamaat-e-Islami* in Pakistan, Maududi developed the most systematic modern articulation of political Islam.

**Key positions:**
- *Hukumiyyah* (divine sovereignty) — Allah alone has sovereignty; democratic sovereignty is *Shirk* in Maududi's framework
- Islamic state as the goal of Islamic political activity
- Comprehensive reinterpretation of Islamic concepts — *Jihad, Khilafah, Islamic economics*

**Influence:** Enormous in South Asia and beyond — his concept of *Hukumiyyah* influenced Sayyid Qutb.

**Criticism:** Many scholars argue his political framework is reductionist — turning Islam's multidimensional guidance into a primarily political ideology.

---

### Sayyid Qutb (1906–1966 CE) — Egypt

**The radical:** Initially a literary critic, Qutb became an Islamist thinker after imprisonment by Nasser's regime. His concept of *Jahiliyyah* (applying the pre-Islamic "age of ignorance" label to contemporary Muslim societies) and his call for *Jihad* against nominally Muslim governments became the intellectual foundation of jihadist movements.

**His influence:** Al-Qaeda and other jihadist groups trace their intellectual lineage to Qutb's later writings — particularly *Milestones* (*Ma'alim fi al-Tariq*).

**The scholarly response:** The overwhelming majority of classical and contemporary Ahlus Sunnah scholars reject Qutb's concepts of declaring Muslim societies as *Jahiliyyah* and armed revolution against Muslim governments. Ibn Baz, Ibn Uthaymeen, and Al-Albani all critiqued Qutbist ideology.

---

### Yusuf al-Qaradawi (1926–2022 CE) — Qatar

**The fiqh of minorities.** One of the most globally influential Islamic scholars of the 20th–21st centuries. He developed the concept of *Fiqh al-Aqalliyyat* — Islamic jurisprudence for Muslim minorities living in non-Muslim countries.

**Key contributions:**
- Muslims in the West can participate in civic and political life
- Many rulings have flexibility based on the principle of *Maqasid al-Sharia*
- Islam is a "middle way" (*Wasatiyyah*) between rigid literalism and liberal reductionism

## 🌐 Islam and Modernity — The Key Debates

Contemporary Islamic thought continues to wrestle with:

| Question | Positions |
|---------|----------|
| **Democracy and Islamic governance** | From rejection (Maududi) to qualified acceptance (Qaradawi) to full embrace (some Tunisian scholars) |
| **Women's leadership and rights** | From classical positions to expanded roles — a genuine ongoing scholarly debate |
| **Religious pluralism** | Does Islam teach exclusivism or can other paths lead to salvation? |
| **Quran and science** | How to handle apparent conflicts between scientific consensus and traditional interpretation |
| **The Caliphate** | Is restoring it a religious obligation? Most contemporary scholars say no |

---

## ✅ Key Takeaways

- Modern Islamic thought emerged as a response to colonialism, internal stagnation, and the challenge of Western modernity.
- Three main responses: revivalism (return to pure sources), reform (reinterpretation within Islamic framework), and modernism (substantial reinterpretation).
- Key figures: Shah Waliullah (synthesis), Ibn Abd al-Wahhab (strict revivalism), al-Afghani (Pan-Islamism), Abduh (rationalist reform), Iqbal (poet-philosopher), al-Banna (political organizing), Maududi (systematic political Islam), Qutb (jihadist ideology — largely rejected), Qaradawi (fiqh of minorities).
- The debates continue — on governance, gender, pluralism, and methodology — and are features of a living tradition, not signs of failure.
