# Classical Islamic Philosophy (Falsafa)

Difficulty: Advanced | Part: Part 18 Advanced Studies

---

## What Is Falsafa?

Falsafa (from the Greek philosophia) is the tradition of Islamic philosophy that emerged from the translation of Greek philosophical texts into Arabic during the Abbasid period (8th-10th centuries CE). Islamic philosophers engaged critically with Aristotle, Plato, and the Neoplatonists accepting, modifying, and rejecting their ideas in dialogue with Islamic revelation.

---

## The Major Figures

**Al-Kindi (801-873 CE)** the first systematic Islamic philosopher. He worked to reconcile Greek philosophy with Islamic theology, argued for the compatibility of reason and revelation, and made significant contributions to mathematics, optics, and music theory.

**Al-Farabi (872-950 CE)** developed the most systematic Islamic political philosophy, drawing on Plato's Republic while grounding it in Islamic governance. His "Al-Madinah al-Fadilah" (The Virtuous City) describes the ideal Islamic polity as one governed by a philosopher-prophet. He also made major contributions to music theory and logic.

**Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980-1037 CE)** the greatest philosopher-physician in Islamic history. His "Kitab al-Shifa" (The Book of Healing) is a comprehensive philosophical encyclopedia. His "Floating Man" thought experiment a person suspended in air with all senses cut off who still knows "I exist" anticipates Descartes' cogito by 600 years. His argument for the Necessary Existent remains one of the most discussed proofs for God's existence in philosophical literature.

**Al-Ghazali (1058-1111 CE)** as a philosopher, al-Ghazali is the great critic of the falsafa tradition. His "Tahafut al-Falasifah" (The Incoherence of the Philosophers) subjected Ibn Sina and al-Farabi to devastating critique, arguing that their positions on the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars, and bodily resurrection were incompatible with Islamic belief and philosophically incoherent.

**Ibn Rushd (Averroes, 1126-1198 CE)** responded to al-Ghazali with "Tahafut al-Tahafut" (The Incoherence of the Incoherence). His commentaries on Aristotle were so comprehensive that he was known in medieval Europe simply as "The Commentator." Ibn Rushd argued for the compatibility of philosophy and revelation and was enormously influential on European scholasticism through his Latin translators.

**Ibn Tufayl (1105-1185 CE)** wrote "Hayy ibn Yaqzan" the story of a child raised in isolation on a deserted island who, through pure reason and observation, arrives at the same philosophical and spiritual truths reached through revelation. This thought experiment influenced European Enlightenment thinking about natural reason.

---

## The Philosopher-Theologian Tension

The tension between the philosophers (falasifa) and the theologians (mutakallimun) is one of the defining intellectual dramas of Islamic civilization. Al-Ghazali's critique effectively ended the classical falsafa tradition within the Islamic world subsequent thinkers either worked within the mutakallim framework or integrated philosophical tools into it rather than operating as independent philosophers.

In Europe, however, the Islamic philosophical tradition particularly Ibn Rushd became foundational. The "double truth" controversy in 13th-century European universities whether philosophical and theological truth could contradict was catalyzed by Averroist philosophy.
